Industrial Damper Problems

Why dampers stick, leak, warp and hunt, and what to do about it

Most industrial damper problems come down to one of four complaints: it won't move, it won't close, it won't hold position, or the actuator keeps failing. On energy from waste, biomass and boiler plant the cause is nearly always heat, ash and dust, or the way the damper and its drive have been linked together.

This guide sets out what we see on site, what to check first and when it is time to replace the damper rather than keep repairing it. It covers multi-blade louvre dampers, butterfly dampers on furnace and exhaust lines, fan inlet vanes and the actuators that drive them.

Always isolate the actuator and make the duct safe before working on a damper. A damper can move without warning when its drive is energised or the gas flow changes.

Damper Stiff or Seized

The most common problem we see

Ash and dust find their way into shaft bearings and glands, and on hot duties shafts and bearings corrode or gall. Thermal growth can also close up the clearance between blades and frame, so a damper that moves freely when cold binds once it is up to temperature. Gland packing that has been over-tightened to stop a leak does the same.

The first check is always the same: disconnect the actuator and move the damper by hand. If the damper is stiff on its own, the problem is the damper. Fitting a bigger actuator to a seized damper just moves the failure somewhere else, usually a sheared key, a bent linkage or a twisted shaft.

Condensation is a big one on exhaust lines. We were asked to look at a six-zone furnace where the exhaust valves kept seizing. Each zone had a modulating air valve and exhaust valve, and the exhaust valves were cast iron body and disc, with a maker's data sheet that said no moisture allowed. During heat-up and cool-down the exhaust gas condenses, mixes with the dust and the valve locks up. The answer there is stainless parts and a swing-through disc that does not need a tight seat. Most exhaust duties do not need tight shut-off, and a disc that swings through has nothing to stick against.

  • Clean out and free the bearings, or fit new ones. Bearings mounted outboard, away from the heat, last much longer.
  • Repack glands to the right tightness, and look at seal air or purged glands on dusty duties.
  • If the damper binds only when hot, check blade clearances against the operating temperature, not the cold setting.
  • If it keeps seizing after repair, the damper is probably not suited to the duty (see repair or replace, below).

Warped Blades and Frames

When the damper isn't built for the heat

Heat is the hardest thing on a damper. Blades that are too thin, the wrong material, or not free to expand will distort, and once they distort the damper binds, passes and breaks its linkages. Temperature cycling makes it worse, as does running above the design temperature during upsets.

Materials matter just as much. On one furnace, aluminium-bodied butterfly valves had been fitted on an exhaust duty up to 350°C, with dust and some moisture at start-up. They had been repaired several times. An aluminium valve on that duty is always going to be living on borrowed time; a stainless swing-through damper built for the temperature is the better fit.

A real example. At a number of UK biomass and energy from waste plants, the original overfire air dampers supplied with the plant failed in the harsh conditions they worked in. They were replaced with AMMTech dampers designed as drop-in replacements, fitting the same ductwork and drives. They have now run for three to four years without any maintenance. The full story is in our biomass damper recommissioning case study.

Damper Passing When Shut

Leaking past the blades

A damper that passes when it should be shut is either worn or not actually closing. Seals burn or wear, blade edges erode on dusty gas, and warped blades no longer meet. Just as often the damper is fine, but the linkage or end position has been set so the blades stop short of closed. If the actuator says 0% and the damper indicator does not, start with the linkage.

It is also worth remembering that a control louvre damper is not an isolation damper. If you need positive isolation, for example to make a duct safe for entry, it needs a damper designed for it, such as a double-seal damper with seal air or a triple-offset design.

Fast-Cycling Dampers

When the seat takes a hammering

Some furnace air and exhaust dampers open and close every 45 seconds or so, switching between cold air and dusty flue gas at 400°C or more. That is over a thousand cycles a day, and it is very hard on the seat.

On one job the existing dampers were metal ledge-seat valves with a heavy ductile iron disc, driven by a large spring-return actuator. The disc has real momentum when it hits the seat, and the seats were getting damaged. The heavier disc also needs more torque, so the actuator gets bigger, which makes the slam worse.

  • Lighter disc. A 316 stainless disc is lighter than ductile iron, so it hits the seat with less force.
  • Soft seat. A braided or elastomer seat, where the temperature allows, absorbs the closing force instead of the metal taking it.
  • Right-sized actuator. The proposed DN200 replacement needed only about 30 Nm to operate. A smaller pneumatic actuator costs less, uses less air on every stroke and slams the disc less.
  • Can be stripped for cleaning. On dusty gas, a damper that comes apart for cleaning is worth a lot at the next outage.

Poor Control and Hunting

Usually the linkage, not the actuator

Poor control is rarely the actuator's fault. The usual causes are:

  • Slack in the linkage. Worn rod ends and loose crank arms give lost movement, and the control loop chases it. Hunting wears out the drive and the damper.
  • Part-stroke use. If the actuator turns through 90° but the linkage only uses a third of that, you have thrown away two thirds of the control resolution.
  • Wrong geometry. A typical damper does most of its flow change in the first part of its opening. The right linkage geometry evens that out so the loop can control across the whole range.

Beck's Link-Assist™ program works out the crank arm lengths and angles for a linkage, so the drive uses its full stroke and the damper responds evenly. At one oil refinery, two Beck 11-209 drives on the FD fan vane and ID fan damper were set up this way in about 2011 and are still running, around 15 years later.

Actuator Failures

Why damper drives burn out

When a damper actuator keeps failing, look at what it is being asked to do before replacing it like for like. The common causes are a damper that is binding or seized, an actuator that was too small for the torque at temperature in the first place, heat radiated from the duct, and constant hunting on a drive whose motor was never meant for continuous modulation.

Beck electric drives are built for exactly this duty. The low-speed, no-burnout motor will not overheat even when stalled or modulating continuously, and the drives are often fitted and forgotten. At several UK biomass plants, every Beck actuator we inspected during damper maintenance was in good condition and went straight back into service. After a Damper Overhaul

Recommission the drive, don't just bolt it back on

When a damper is overhauled the actuator has to come off and go back on, and that is where a lot of new problems start. Before the plant returns to service:

  • Reconnect the linkage and crank arms to the original settings, or set them again properly.
  • Set the closed and open end positions against the damper itself, not the old marks.
  • Run the damper through its full stroke and check nothing binds or overruns.
  • Check the control signal and position feedback match what the damper is actually doing.

Repair or Replace?

When to stop patching an old damper

Repair makes sense when the frame and blades are sound and the problem is bearings, seals or linkage. We can supply damper spares, including parts for older makes, from drawings, samples or photographs.

Replacement is usually the better answer when blades or frames are distorted, when the same damper fails every outage, or when the original maker no longer supports it. A drop-in replacement built to the existing flange and duct dimensions avoids changing the ductwork, and the existing actuator can often be reused.

Check the fit before you order. Older plant is full of non-standard sizes, and these are the things that catch people out:

  • Face to face. Old valves were often thicker than today's standard wafer pattern. We have seen 63 mm and 100 mm bodies, and spool pieces fitted to fill the gap when a thinner valve went in.
  • Body outside diameter. A wafer valve centres itself on the flange bolts. A standard PN body between ANSI flanges can be too small to centre properly, so measure the old one.
  • Shaft and actuator drive. Square, star and double-D drives are not interchangeable, and a big actuator may not fit a smaller replacement shaft. Note the mounting flange (e.g. F07/F12) and drive size.
  • Limit switches and positioners. Sites often want to keep the limit box or actuator they already stock, so check what can be carried over.

Send us photos of the old damper, its nameplate and the measurements above and we will check what fits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my damper stick?

Usually ash or dust in the shaft bearings, corrosion on hot duties, or blades binding in the frame as they expand with heat. Over-tight glands are another common cause. Disconnect the actuator and move the damper by hand to confirm the damper is the problem.

Why is my damper leaking when it is closed?

Worn or burnt seals, warped or eroded blades, or a linkage and end position set so the blades never reach fully closed. Check the actuator's closed position against the blades first.

Why does my exhaust damper keep seizing?

Often condensation. During heat-up and cool-down the exhaust gas condenses and mixes with dust, and cast iron parts corrode and lock up. Stainless parts and a swing-through disc that does not need a tight seat are usually the cure.

Why does the damper actuator keep failing?

Most often because the damper is binding, the actuator is too small for the torque at temperature, it is exposed to heat from the duct, or it is hunting continuously on a motor not rated for modulating duty.

Can you replace an obsolete damper without changing the ductwork?

Yes. AMMTech dampers can be built as drop-in replacements to the existing flange and duct dimensions, and the existing actuator can often be reused.

Do you supply damper spares?

Yes. Blades, shafts, bearings, seals, linkages and actuators, including parts for older dampers where the original maker no longer supports them.
