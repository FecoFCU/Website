Butterfly Valve Guide

How they work, the main types and how to choose one

A butterfly valve is one of the simplest and most widely used valves in industry. It is light, compact and inexpensive for its size, and it can isolate a line or regulate flow. This guide explains how butterfly valves work, the main types, where they are a good choice and where they are not, and what to check before you buy one.

On this page: How it works · Types · Butterfly vs ball and gate valves · Seat materials · Operation · How to choose · Installation tips · Questions

How a Butterfly Valve Works

A butterfly valve has a round disc mounted on a shaft across the bore of the valve. Turning the shaft a quarter turn (90 degrees) swings the disc from fully closed, where it seals against the seat, to fully open, where it lies edge-on to the flow.

In most general-purpose valves the seat is a rubber liner that lines the body and also seals against the pipe flanges, so no separate flange gaskets are needed. Only the disc and the liner touch the fluid, which is why the body can be ductile iron while the disc and liner are chosen for the medium.

Because the disc can be held part-open, a butterfly valve can also regulate flow, especially between about 30 and 70 degrees open.

Types of Butterfly Valve

Most industrial butterfly valves are concentric, resilient-seated valves: the shaft passes through the centre of the disc and the disc seals against a rubber or PTFE liner. They differ mainly in how they fit into the pipeline.

TypeHow it fitsTypical use
WaferClamped between two pipe flanges by long bolts or studs passing outside the valve bodyThe most common and economical type for general isolation and control
LugThreaded lugs around the body; each flange is bolted to the valve separatelyWhere the pipe on one side must be removed while the valve stays in place
Double flangedIts own flanges, bolted to the pipe flanges like a section of pipeLarge sizes, water mains and heavy-duty lines
PTFE-linedWafer or lug body with a PTFE liner and an encapsulated discAcids, chlorine, solvents and other corrosive or high-purity fluids
High-performance (offset)Disc offset from the shaft so it lifts off the seat as it opensHigher pressures and temperatures, steam and hydrocarbons

Butterfly Valve vs Ball Valve vs Gate Valve

The butterfly valve's big advantages are size and weight. A butterfly valve is a fraction of the weight and face-to-face length of a gate or globe valve of the same size, which makes it cheaper to buy, easier to support and quicker to install, and the difference grows with pipe size.

Weight and lengthLightest and shortestHeavier and longer in large sizesHeaviest and longest
Cost in large sizesLowestHighHigh

Pigging or rodding the lineNot possiblePossible with full borePossible
Best forWater, air, gases, chemicals and powders in medium to large sizes at moderate pressureSmaller sizes, higher pressures, tight shut-offIsolation where full bore is needed

Butterfly valves are less suited to very high pressures and temperatures (unless a high-performance design is used), to lines that need a clear bore for pigging, and to throttling at very small openings, where cavitation and wear can occur.

Seat Materials

The seat (liner) is the part most likely to fail if it is wrongly chosen. Select it for the fluid first, then check the temperature: a liner run close to its limit ages much faster.

Ways to Operate a Butterfly Valve

  • Lever: simple and quick, usually up to about DN200 to DN300, with notched positions for regulating
  • Gearbox and handwheel: for larger valves or where the flow pushes on the disc; self-locking, with a position indicator
  • Chain wheel: a gearbox with a chain-driven wheel for valves mounted high up
  • Pneumatic actuator: fast on/off or, with a positioner, modulating control; spring-return versions fail safe on loss of air
  • Electric actuator: on/off or modulating from a 4-20 mA signal, for example Beck electric actuators for precise, continuous control

Valves with an ISO 5211 top flange can be motorised later by swapping the lever or gearbox for a standard actuator.

How to Choose a Butterfly Valve

What to have ready when you ask for a price

  • Size (DN or inches) and the flange standard: EN 1092 PN10/PN16, ANSI Class 150 or BS 10 Table D/E. See the butterfly valve size chart
  • Medium: what flows through the valve, including any solids or chemicals
  • Pressure and temperature: normal and maximum
  • Duty: isolation, regulating, or both; how often it operates
  • Body style: wafer, lug (essential if the valve may be at the end of a line) or flanged
  • Materials: body, disc and seat to suit the medium
  • Operation: lever, gearbox, chain wheel or actuator, and the control signal for an actuator
  • Special requirements: ATEX zone, drinking water, food contact, or certificates

Installation Tips

  • Insert the valve with the disc slightly open, so the disc edge sits inside the body and is not damaged by the flanges
  • Do not fit extra gaskets with rubber-lined valves: the liner is the flange seal
  • Centre the valve between the flanges before tightening, and tighten the bolts evenly in a cross pattern
  • Check the disc can open fully without touching the pipe, a reducer, a check valve or another fitting next to it
  • Where possible, keep a few pipe diameters of straight pipe upstream of a regulating valve, and fit large valves with the shaft horizontal on dirty or slurry duties
  • Never weld the pipe near a valve that is already fitted: heat damages the liner

Always follow the manufacturer's installation instructions for the specific valve.

Butterfly Valve Questions

What is a butterfly valve used for?

Isolating and regulating the flow of liquids, gases and powders, mainly in medium to large pipe sizes. Typical uses include water and wastewater, HVAC, chemical plants, power stations, food and drink, and bulk powder handling.

Can a butterfly valve be used for flow control?

Yes. A butterfly valve can be held part-open to regulate flow, typically between about 30 and 70 degrees open. With a positioner-equipped actuator it can provide continuous modulating control.

Which way round should a butterfly valve be installed?

Most concentric, rubber-lined butterfly valves are bi-directional and can be installed either way round. Check the manufacturer's instructions for any flow direction marked on the valve.

What is the difference between a wafer and a lug butterfly valve?

A wafer valve is clamped between two flanges by long bolts. A lug valve has threaded lugs so each flange is bolted to the valve separately, allowing the pipe on one side to be removed with the valve still in place.

Do butterfly valves need gaskets?

Rubber-lined butterfly valves do not: the liner seals against the pipe flanges. Fitting extra gaskets can damage the liner and stop the disc sealing.

How long does a butterfly valve last?

With the right seat material and correct installation, a butterfly valve can last many years. Most failures come from a liner that does not suit the fluid or temperature, or from damage during installation.
