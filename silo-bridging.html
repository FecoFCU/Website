Silo and Hopper Bridging Guide

Causes and solutions for bridging, ratholing and blocked hoppers

A silo or hopper that will not discharge stops everything downstream. Operators end up hammering the vessel or rodding it from above, production waits, and the problem comes back the next time the material settles.

This guide explains why bulk materials bridge and rathole, how to work out what is happening in your vessel, and the options for fixing it, from changes to the vessel itself to flow aids fitted from outside. What Is Silo Bridging?

The common flow problems in silos and hoppers

Bridging, also called arching, is when material forms a stable arch across the hopper outlet. The vessel can be full, but nothing comes out. There are two kinds:

  • Cohesive arch: fine, damp or sticky particles bind together strongly enough to span the outlet. Common with soil, clay, damp sand, compost, fine powders and filter cake.
  • Mechanical (interlocking) arch: large, flat or fibrous particles lock together over the opening. Common with wood chip, biomass, shredded material and lump products when the outlet is too small for the particle size.

Other flow problems often appear alongside bridging, or are mistaken for it:

  • Ratholing: material flows only through a narrow channel above the outlet, leaving stagnant material packed around the walls. Once the channel empties, flow stops, even though most of the vessel is still full.
  • Erratic flow: discharge swings between a trickle and a surge as arches and ratholes form and collapse.
  • Flooding: a fine powder that has been aerated, often after a rathole collapses, rushes out uncontrollably like a liquid.
  • Wall build-up: material sticks to the walls and cone and hardens over time, reducing usable capacity and eventually narrowing the outlet.

Mass Flow and Funnel Flow

Why some vessels rathole and others do not

How material moves inside a vessel decides which problems you are likely to get.

  • Mass flow: all the material moves whenever any is drawn off. It needs a hopper with walls steep and smooth enough for the material to slide on them. Mass flow vessels discharge first-in, first-out and do not rathole, though a cohesive material can still bridge if the outlet is too small.
  • Funnel flow (core flow): material moves down a central channel while material near the walls stays put. Funnel flow is common because shallower cones are cheaper and take less headroom, but it leads to ratholes, stale material that sits in the vessel for weeks, and segregation of mixed products.

Many existing silos and hoppers were designed for a different material, or for no particular material at all, so they run in funnel flow. The fix is either to change the vessel so it runs closer to mass flow, or to keep the material near the walls moving by other means.

Working Out What Is Happening

Questions to ask before choosing a fix

The right silo bridging solution depends on what is actually going on in the vessel. Before spending money, it helps to answer these questions:

  • When does it happen? After a stop, in certain weather, with a particular supplier's material, or all the time?
  • Where does it hang up? An arch at the outlet, a hole down the middle with material on the walls, or build-up on the cone?
  • What has changed? A new material, a different moisture content, a larger fill level, or a new feeder or gate?
  • What has been tried? Vibrators, air cannons, aeration pads or hammering, and what happened when they were used?
  • What are the constraints? Headroom, budget, downtime, food hygiene, ATEX zones and the compressed air available.

For a new vessel, laboratory flow-property testing of the material (shear cell testing) gives the hopper angle and outlet size needed for reliable flow. For an existing vessel, the answers above, a drawing of the vessel and a few photos are usually enough to identify the cause and the options.

Silo Bridging Solutions

The main ways to fix a blocked silo or hopper

SolutionWhat it involvesPoints to consider
Change the vesselSteepen the cone, enlarge the outlet, or fit an insert to convert funnel flow towards mass flow.Tackles the root cause, but means fabrication, downtime and often lost capacity or headroom.
Low-friction linersLine the cone with UHMW polyethylene or polished stainless steel so material slides more easily.Helps where wall friction is the problem; less effective on very damp or sticky material, and liners wear.

VibratorsShake the vessel wall to dislodge material.Simple to fit, but noisy, can fatigue the vessel, and can compact damp or cohesive material, making bridging worse.

Aeration pads and fluidisersLow-pressure air through pads or discs aerates powder near the wall.Suits light, dry powders; many units may be needed, and it can cause flooding.
Mechanical dischargersBin activators, live-bottom screws or agitators drive material out of the base.Positive discharge, but a larger investment with moving parts and changes to the vessel.

Hammering and rodding are the most common short-term fixes, and the most costly over time. Hammering dents the vessel, which creates ledges where material builds up and makes the problem worse. Rodding from above often means working at height or over moving plant.

Never enter a silo or hopper to clear a blockage. A bridge or rathole can collapse without warning and bury anyone inside or on top of the material. Silos are confined spaces, and clearing should always be done from outside the vessel.

Silo Bridging Questions

How do you stop silo bridging?

Either change the vessel so material can flow freely, by steepening the cone, enlarging the outlet or lining the walls, or keep the material near the walls moving so an arch cannot form, using a flow aid such as AirSweep. Which is right depends on the material, the vessel and how it is used.

What is the difference between bridging and ratholing?

Bridging is an arch of material across the outlet, so nothing flows. Ratholing is a narrow flow channel above the outlet with stagnant material left around the walls, so flow stops once the channel empties.

Can vibrators make silo bridging worse?

Yes. Vibration can compact damp or cohesive materials such as soil, clay and some powders, making the bridge stronger. It can also fatigue welds and the vessel wall over time.

Is it safe to enter a silo to clear a blockage?

No. A bridge or rathole can collapse without warning and bury anyone inside or on top of the material. Silos are confined spaces, and blockages should be cleared from outside the vessel.

Can bridging be fixed without modifying the silo?

Often, yes. Flow aids such as AirSweep are fitted through the vessel wall and keep material moving without changing the vessel's shape. Nozzles can be fitted to steel, concrete, fibreglass and wooden vessels.

Which materials are most likely to bridge?

Damp, sticky or fine materials such as soil, clay, compost, damp sand, filter cake and fine powders form cohesive arches. Flat, long or fibrous materials such as wood chip and biomass form interlocking arches over small outlets.
