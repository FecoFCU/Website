Scoop-Controlled Fluid Couplings

Faults, scoop control upgrades and spares

Scoop-controlled fluid couplings give variable speed from a constant-speed motor, and many have been running on boiler feed pumps, fans, mills and conveyors for decades. Keeping them running means knowing what the common faults look like, keeping the scoop control reliable, and finding spares for couplings that are no longer supported by their original manufacturer.

IPT Engineering supports scoop-controlled couplings from Fluidrive, Vulcan Sinclair, Pembril and others. Our experience of these machines goes back to working for Fluidrive and Voith, and includes the set-up and recommissioning of a large Fluidrive scoop coupling driving a power station boiler feed pump.

New to how they work? Our guide to how fluid couplings work explains scoop-filling and scoop-trimming couplings step by step. This page is about keeping them in service. For fixed-fill FCU, SFU and STC couplings, see our fluid coupling fault finding guide.

Where Scoop Couplings Are Used

Variable speed from a fixed-speed motor

Moving the scoop tube changes how much oil is in the coupling's working circuit, and so the output speed, while the motor runs at constant speed. That gives a soft, no-load motor start and stepless speed control. Typical applications include:

  • Power stations: boiler feed pumps, and ID and FD fans
  • Steel works: large fans such as BOS and Kish extraction fans
  • Refineries and process plant: pumps, including hot charge pumps
  • Minerals and building materials: mills and crushers
  • Bulk handling: long and heavily loaded conveyors

On fans and pumps, a scoop coupling typically gives stable speed control down to around 20% of full speed; on constant-torque loads such as mills and crushers, down to around a third.

SCR Scoop Coupling Fault Finding

Symptoms, possible causes and what to do

These notes cover Fluidrive SCR scoop-filling couplings (SCR24R and SCR25W sizes 18 to 41, and SCR25 and SCR25K sizes 46 and 52), drawn from the original maintenance documentation and our own experience. The older SCR 4 and SCR 5, and Vulcan Sinclair, Pembril and other scoop-filling couplings, work on the same principle, so most of it applies to them too.

Many faults come back to three things: the oil fill, whether the oil is circulating freely, and the valves and nozzles that control flow out of the working circuit. Always isolate the drive before opening the coupling, and check the oil fill with the scoop fully out.

Will not reach full speedCoupling underfilled; oil not circulating because of blocked pipe connections, blanking plates left in the cooler flanges or wrong direction of rotation; emptying valves dirty, worn or not seating, or their feed pipes blocked; a leak-off nozzle or valve missing; emptying valve spring rating too high for the motor speed; worn scoop tip ball seating or a failed key in the scoop operating rod; filling tundish nut loose or tundish valve not closing; low motor speed, an under-rated motor, or extra resistance in the driven machine.Check the oil fill with the scoop fully out. Check pipework and coolers for blanks and blockages, and confirm rotation. Remove and clean the valves and check their seatings. Check the scoop tip, operating rod and tundish. Check motor speed and the driven machine.
Will not come down in speedScoop not moving because of a control or actuator fault; coupling overfilled; leak-off nozzles or holes blocked, or valves dirty; diaphragm emptying valves jammed during assembly.Check the scoop control first: is the scoop actually moving? Then check the fill, and remove and clean the leak-off nozzles and valves.
Overheating (oil continuously above 88°C / 190°F)Cooling water supply off, drains blocked, cooler choked on the water side, or cooler water boxes wrongly assembled; on air-cooled units, dirty radiator fins or a fan not working; incorrect oil fill; oil not circulating or only partly circulating because of blanks or unpierced joints in the pipework, pipework bore too small or too many 90° bends, leak-off nozzles choked or burred over, or dirty valves; filling tundish nut loose or spindle broken; coupling internals choked with carbon.Check cooling water flow, drains and cooler, or the radiator and fan. Oil thrown off a rotating casing can land well away from the leak, so trace it back carefully with the drive stopped.
Oil leaking around the output shaftCoupling overfilled, including by water leaking from the cooler into the oil; damaged scoop housing or oil retaining ring joint; scoop housing shroud damaged or not fitted; on the SCR25, outboard bearing oil feed nozzle missing or drilled oversize, or bearing drain ports blocked; a large-diameter half coupling fitted. Leaking only on shutdown points to a damaged oil retaining ring, or braking applied to the reservoir casing.Check the fill and look for water in the oil. Remove the scoop housing bracket and remake the joint. Check the shroud, the bearing oil feed nozzle and the drain ports.

A note on heat: slip losses in the coupling become heat in the oil. On fan and pump drives they are greatest at around two-thirds of full speed, at roughly 15% of rated power, so a coupling that now spends most of its time in that range, perhaps because the plant runs at part load, will run hotter than when it was specified. It is worth knowing before chasing the cooler or the oil.

Scoop Control Upgrades

Replacing obsolete and unreliable scoop actuators

The scoop actuator sets the speed of the whole drive, so a worn, obsolete or unreliable actuator shows up straight away as poor speed control or a drive that will not respond. Many original scoop actuators, both pneumatic and electric, are now obsolete, with no spares available.

As UK agent for Beck electric actuators, IPT has re-engineered scoop control on fluid couplings including:

  • Boiler feed pump fluid coupling control at power stations
  • BOS and Kish fan fluid coupling control at a steel works
  • A hot charge pump fluid coupling at a refinery
  • A mill fluid coupling at a plasterboard plant, where the original actuator failed repeatedly under heavy vibration (read the case study)

Beck actuators are built for continuous modulation, with a no-burnout motor and spur gearing that virtually eliminates wear-induced backlash, which suits a scoop that is adjusted constantly. We review the existing linkage and mounting, the control signal and the stroke, and design the installation so the new actuator fits the existing scoop lever. See our actuator services for more.

Older Units: SCR 4 and SCR 5

The back story, and why the contract number matters

Many scoop couplings still running today are Fluidrive SCR 4 and SCR 5 units, the imperial predecessors of the SCR 24 and SCR 25. Production started around contract number C8000, in the mid-1950s, and carried on alongside the newer metric range until the early 1970s. Knowing which version you have is the key to getting the right parts.

  • SCR 4 or SCR 5? On an even-numbered type such as the SCR 4, the coupling is supported by the motor and the driven machine. On an odd-numbered type such as the SCR 5, the scoop housing bracket carries an outboard bearing, used where the driven shaft cannot carry the coupling's weight or on extended gearbox shafts carrying brake drums.
  • Imperial build: SCR 4 and 5 units use BSF threads and fasteners, and a taper shaft for the runner half coupling.
  • Higher-torque versions: the SCR 4K and SCR 5K have twin keyways in the runner shaft half coupling and double the number of runner bolts.
  • Scoop housing bracket: fabricated steel on early units, changed to cast iron from around C18500, and from around C19500 to the cast iron design with alignment lugs on its front face that carried on into the SCR 24.
  • Scoop tubes: early tubes were shorter and made for one direction of rotation. Longer dual-rotation tubes, with a spring-assisted return to circuit empty as a fail-safe, became standard from around C24000, and can generally replace the older types.
  • Emptying valves: each SCR 4 or 5 was built for one valve type (diaphragm quick-emptying valves, centrifugal quick-emptying valves or leak-off nozzles), and the back casing only accepts that type. The diaphragm valve was redesigned around C20000, and the two designs are not interchangeable.

In the late 1960s the range was redesigned to metric as the SCR 24 and SCR 25, with a one-piece cast aluminium impeller, revised valves, metric bearings and a stiffer reservoir casing. The back casing now accepts any of the three valve types. The higher-torque SCR 24R and 25R, with double the runner bolts and a parallel runner half coupling, became the standard units. Shaft ends and the input and output mountings were kept the same, so an SCR 24 or 25 can replace an SCR 4 or 5 without disturbing the motor or the driven machine.

The contract number is the key. Design changes were phased in rather than made at a single cut-off, and units of different ages do not always share parts. The contract number on the nameplate (for example C12345A, with a letter for each unit on the same order) identifies the original build, and gives a good idea of the coupling's age:

Scoop Coupling Questions

How does a scoop-controlled fluid coupling vary speed?

A movable scoop tube controls how much oil is in the coupling's working circuit. More oil transmits more torque and the output speeds up; less oil increases slip and the output slows, while the motor runs at constant speed.

What is the difference between scoop-filling and scoop-trimming couplings?

In a scoop-filling coupling, the scoop tube picks up oil from a rotating reservoir and feeds it into the working circuit. In a scoop-trimming coupling, a pump fills the working circuit and the scoop tube trims oil out of it. Scoop-trimming couplings are usually the larger, higher-power units.

Why does my scoop coupling run hot?

The most common causes are poor cooling water flow or a choked cooler, an incorrect oil fill, and oil not circulating freely because of blockages, blanks left in the pipework, or choked leak-off nozzles and valves. Long running at around two-thirds of full speed, where slip losses are highest on fan and pump drives, also raises the oil temperature. On an SCR, oil running continuously above 88°C (190°F) should be investigated.

Why won't my scoop coupling reach full speed?

Usually because the coupling is underfilled, the oil is not circulating, or the emptying valves are dirty, worn or not seating, so oil escapes from the working circuit. Check the fill with the scoop fully out, then the pipework, the valves and the scoop itself. A low motor speed or extra load from the driven machine can have the same effect.

Why won't my scoop coupling come down in speed?

Check first that the scoop is actually moving: a failed actuator or control signal is a common cause. After that, look for an overfilled coupling or blocked leak-off nozzles that stop oil leaving the working circuit.

How old is my Fluidrive scoop coupling?

The contract number on the nameplate gives a good guide. As examples, C8000 is around 1955 to 1956, C20000 around 1963, C30000 around 1970, C50000 around 1980, and numbers starting BRC are from 1990 onwards. The contract number also identifies the original build specification.

Can an SCR 24 or 25 replace an old SCR 4 or 5?

Generally yes. Fluidrive kept the shaft ends and the input and output mountings the same when it introduced the metric SCR 24 and 25, so a newer unit can replace an SCR 4 or 5 without changes to the motor or driven machine. Internal parts are a different matter, so check the contract number before ordering spares.

Can an obsolete scoop actuator be replaced?

Yes. IPT replaces obsolete and unreliable scoop actuators with Beck electric actuators, designing the mounting and linkage to suit the existing scoop lever and control signal.

Can you still get spares for Fluidrive scoop couplings?

Yes. IPT supplies scoop tubes, motor bosses, centre spigots, gland assemblies, seal kits, driving plates and multi disc kits for Fluidrive, Vulcan Sinclair and Pembril couplings, including older units such as the SCR24R and SCR25W.
